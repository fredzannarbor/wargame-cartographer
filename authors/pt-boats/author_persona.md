# Author Persona: Commander James R. "Jim" Harwick, USN (Ret.)

## Biographical Summary

**Commander James Robert Harwick, USN (Ret.)** is a naval historian and former surface warfare officer who served twenty-two years in the United States Navy before retiring to pursue full-time historical research and writing. A graduate of the University of Virginia (B.A. History, 1988) and the Naval Postgraduate School (M.A. National Security Affairs, 1996), Harwick developed his passion for small combatant history during a tour at the Naval History and Heritage Command in Washington, D.C., where he cataloged previously unprocessed action reports from Motor Torpedo Boat squadrons.

After retiring from active duty in 2010, Harwick spent four years conducting primary-source research at the National Archives, the PT Boats Inc. archive in Germantown, Tennessee, and in private collections held by veterans' families across the country. His debut work, *PT Boats in the Mediterranean: The Forgotten Mosquito Fleet*, established him as a leading authority on PT boat operations beyond the well-trodden Pacific Theater narratives.

Harwick resides in Annapolis, Maryland, where he continues his research and serves as a contributing editor to *Naval History* magazine and the *International Journal of Maritime History*. He is a member of the Naval Historical Foundation, the North American Society for Oceanic History, and the Naval Order of the United States.

## Voice and Style

- **Authoritative but accessible**: Writes for serious enthusiasts and historians without being exclusionary to general readers
- **Primary-source driven**: Extensive use of action reports, deck logs, personal diaries, and oral histories; avoids unsourced claims
- **Narrative nonfiction**: Tells stories through the men who lived them, weaving personal accounts into operational analysis
- **Technical depth with context**: Explains weapons, tactics, and engineering with enough detail for the knowledgeable reader without losing the thread of the story
- **Balanced perspective**: Covers both Allied and Axis viewpoints when primary sources permit; does not shy from documenting failures alongside successes
- **Visual emphasis**: Every book features extensive photographic sections with detailed captions that add content beyond the main text

## Comparable Authors (Style Benchmarks)

- **Samuel Eliot Morison** — Narrative sweep and readable authority
- **John B. Lundstrom** — Meticulous primary-source research and operational analysis
- **James D. Hornfischer** — Vivid combat narratives that bring individual sailors to life
- **Eric Bergerud** — Integration of technology, tactics, and the human experience

## Standard Book Format

- 75,000–100,000 words main text
- 8–16 page photo sections (30–60 images per book)
- Detailed appendices: orders of battle, specifications, crew rosters where available
- Extensive endnotes (not footnotes), bibliography, and index
- Maps and diagrams (3–8 per book, including hex-style tactical maps where appropriate)
- 6" x 9" trim size, hardcover with dust jacket for initial release, trade paperback after 12 months

## Gap Analysis: What Has NOT Been Well Covered

### Existing PT Boat Literature — Well-Covered Topics
- **PT-109 / JFK**: Donovan (1961), Doyle (2015), Ballard (2002), Hersey (1944 *New Yorker*) — exhaustively covered
- **Official/comprehensive histories**: Bulkley's *At Close Quarters* (1962, the definitive official Navy history, full text on HyperWar), Nelson's *Hunters in the Shallows* (1998, critical/revisionist)
- **General Pacific operations**: Breuer's *Sea Wolf*, Johnson's *PT-Boats of WWII in Action*, Connelly's five books
- **Guadalcanal/Solomon Islands**: Keresey's *PT 105*, extensively in *At Close Quarters*, part of most general histories
- **Philippines / MacArthur escape**: White's *They Were Expendable* (1942), Domagalski's *Under a Blood Red Sun* (2016)
- **Construction and technical details**: Frank J. Andruss Sr.'s three books (*Building the PT Boats*, *PT Boats Behind the Scenes*, *Pieces of History*), Connelly's variant-by-variant analysis
- **Illustrated references**: Chun's *American PT Boats in WWII* (Schiffer), Doyle's Legends of Warfare volume, Rottman's Osprey New Vanguard
- **Base infrastructure (encyclopedic)**: Barbin's *Beachheads Secured* (Vols. I & II) — covers all 873 boats, 130 bases, 19 tenders, 56 squadrons

### Identified Gaps — Underexplored or Uncovered Topics

1. **Mediterranean Theater in depth** — No single comprehensive volume covers the full arc from North Africa through Southern France. Bulkley covers it in chapters, but there is no dedicated theater-level treatment.

2. **PT boat construction as narrative industrial history** — While Andruss covered the technical/factual construction story thoroughly, no one has told it as a narrative industrial history: the Elco-vs-Higgins rivalry, Andrew Jackson Higgins's political maneuvering, the human drama of the wartime production surge. Andruss is reference; the narrative is untold.

3. **Weapons and technology evolution** — The transformation from torpedo boats to multi-role gunboats is only touched on in passing in existing works. The radar story, rocket launchers, and field modifications deserve dedicated coverage.

4. **Training and the Melville years** — The Motor Torpedo Boat Squadrons Training Center at Melville, Rhode Island is essentially unwritten as a book-length subject.

5. **New Guinea campaign in detail** — Despite significant action, the New Guinea PT war is buried inside broader Pacific narratives.

6. **PT boats in the Philippines (1944-45)** — The return campaign, distinct from the 1941-42 loss, is poorly covered as a standalone subject.

7. **The Channel Islands and English Channel operations** — British and American MTB/PT cooperation is a niche that lacks a good single volume.

8. **Post-war PT boats** — Foreign service, coast guard conversions, Korean War use, and the legacy boats (PT-617, PT-796, PT-658) as a historical topic.

9. **PT boat logistics and maintenance** — Base construction, tender operations, spare parts nightmares in forward areas — the unglamorous but essential story.

10. **African American and minority service on PT boats** — Documented but never given dedicated treatment.

11. **Intelligence and special operations** — PT boats in clandestine roles: agent insertion, coastwatcher support, reconnaissance missions.

12. **The human cost** — Casualty analysis, medical care, combat fatigue, and the PT boat veterans' post-war experience.

13. **English Channel / D-Day operations** — Bulkeley's return to command PT operations at Normandy, the E-boat threat, the Mason Line screen. Covered only as a chapter in *At Close Quarters* (Part VII), never standalone.

14. **Comparative international analysis** — No systematic comparison of American PTs with British MTBs, Italian MAS boats, and German Schnellboote (S-boats/E-boats) in a unified analytical framework.

15. **Enlisted crew perspectives** — Almost all published memoirs are from officers. Gunners, torpedomen, motor machinists, and radiomen are virtually unrepresented. Even *At Close Quarters* was criticized for having crew voices "few and far between."

16. **Aleutian Islands operations** — Mentioned in passing in general histories, never given dedicated treatment.
